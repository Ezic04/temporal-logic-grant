## About the Project

In philosophy and logic, there exist theories that aim to describe a vision of time in which the future is undetermined, meaning that, at present, it is not fixed which situations will occur. This conception of time resonates strongly with common intuitions and underpins many worldviews opposed to a fatalistic picture of reality. Contemporary metaphysical theories, developed in connection with modern physics, also adopt this perspective, treating time as a branching tree: the past is linear and fixed, but the present may lead to several possible futures. The project aims to design new logical tools that enable a more thorough exploration of such conceptions of time.


The first steps toward logical systems capable of modelling branching time were taken in the 1950s by Arthur Prior, now regarded as the founder of modern temporal logic. Although his motivations were philosophical, the logics of branching time—most notably $\CTL$ and $\CTLs$-are studied primarily in computer science today. However, following Prior’s original intention, these logics have a philosophically rich background and reflect two different ways of understanding an open future: the Peircean and the Ockhamist view. According to the first one, statements about future events can be true now only if they would hold “no matter how things unfold”; according to the second one, the truth of such statements depends on which possible future we treat as relevant.


The project is motivated by this philosophical heritage, as well as by the fact that the existing deductive systems for $\CTL$ and $\CTLs$ are, for theoretical reasons, too weak to be applied within broader, non-logical theories—including philosophical ones. It turns out, however, that for the logics we study, it is possible to build stronger systems that allow one to prove any statement that logically follows from a given set of premises. The project focuses on constructing such systems for four logics of branching time: $\TLR$ (basic reachability logic), $\UB$ (universal branching), and the widely used $\CTL$ and $\CTLs$ (computational tree logics). These new systems will employ inference rules that operate on infinite sets of premises—hence the name “infinitary systems”. The innovative character of the project lies not only in designing new systems but also in introducing methodological tools that have not previously been used in the construction of infinitary proof systems. Interest in such systems is currently growing within the broader field of other modal logics.


The project also has an important philosophical component. To justify the possibility of using infinite inference rules, we appeal to the dispositional conception of belief, according to which a person does not need to individually consider every premise in order to accept a general conclusion—it is enough that he or she possesses an appropriate disposition to accept each instance. We also investigate how our systems can support the study of contemporary philosophical theories that treat time as branching, such as S. McCall’s approach or the $\sf{BST}$ (branching space-time) theories initiated by N. Belnap.


The overarching goal of the project is to demonstrate that modern logical tools can effectively contribute to ongoing philosophical discussions about the nature of time, including debates on determinism, the purposefulness of the world, and human free will.
